## Chapter 1: Research Methodology: An Introduction
### Meaning of Research
*   **Definition**: A scientific and systematic search for pertinent information on a specific topic [20].
*   **Concept**: It is an art of scientific investigation, a "systematized effort to gain new knowledge" [20], and an original contribution to the existing stock of knowledge making for its advancement [21].
*   **Essence**: A movement from the known to the unknown; a voyage of discovery [20].

### Objectives of Research
*   **Core Purpose**: To discover answers to questions through the application of scientific procedures; to find out the truth which is hidden and has not been discovered as yet [23].
*   **Classifications**:
    1.  *Exploratory or Formulative*: Gain familiarity with a phenomenon or achieve new insights into it [23].
    2.  *Descriptive*: Portray accurately the characteristics of a particular individual, situation, or group [24].
    3.  *Diagnostic*: Determine the frequency with which something occurs or with which it is associated with something else [24].
    4.  *Hypothesis-Testing*: Test a hypothesis of a causal relationship between variables [24].

### Motivation in Research
*   **Key Drivers**:
    *   Desire to get a research degree along with its consequential benefits [24].
    *   Desire to face the challenge in solving the unsolved problems [24].
    *   Desire to get intellectual joy of doing some creative work [24].
    *   Desire to be of service to society [24].
    *   Desire to get respectability [24].

### Types of Research
```text
                          TYPES OF RESEARCH
                                  │
    ┌─────────────────┬───────────┴───────────┬─────────────────┐
    ▼                 ▼                       ▼                 ▼
Descriptive vs.   Applied vs.            Quantitative vs.  Conceptual vs.
 Analytical       Fundamental             Qualitative       Empirical
 (Ex-post facto)  (Problem-solving vs.    (Measurement vs.   (Theory vs.
                  Theory building)        Attitude/Motives)  Data-driven)
```

*   **Descriptive vs. Analytical**:
    *   *Descriptive*: Surveys and fact-finding enquiries of different kinds. The researcher has no control over variables; they can only report what has happened or is happening (Ex-post facto research) [25].
    *   *Analytical*: The researcher has to use facts or information already available, and analyze these to make a critical evaluation [25].
*   **Applied vs. Fundamental**:
    *   *Applied*: Aims at finding a solution for an immediate problem facing a society or an industrial/business organisation [25].
    *   *Fundamental (Basic/Pure)*: Mainly concerned with generalizations and with the formulation of a theory ("gathering knowledge for knowledge's sake") [25].
*   **Quantitative vs. Qualitative**:
    *   *Quantitative*: Based on the measurement of quantity or amount; applicable to phenomena that can be expressed in terms of quantity [26].
    *   *Qualitative*: Concerned with qualitative phenomenon, i.e., involving quality or kind (e.g., Motivation Research to investigate reasons for human behavior using projective techniques) [26].
*   **Conceptual vs. Empirical**:
    *   *Conceptual*: Related to some abstract idea(s) or theory; generally used by philosophers and thinkers to develop new concepts or to reinterpret existing ones [27].
    *   *Empirical (Experimental)*: Relies on experience or observation alone, often without due regard for system and theory. It is database-driven, coming up with conclusions which are capable of being verified by observation or experiment [27].
*   **Other Types**:
    *   *One-time vs. Longitudinal*: Confined to a single time-period vs. carried on over several time-periods [27].
    *   *Field-setting vs. Laboratory vs. Simulation*: Based on the environment in which it is carried out [27].
    *   *Clinical vs. Diagnostic*: Case-study or indepth approaches to reach basic causal relations [27].
    *   *Exploratory vs. Formalized*: Development of hypotheses vs. substantial structure with specific hypotheses to be tested [27].
    *   *Historical*: Utilizes historical sources like documents, remains, etc. to study events/ideas of the past [27].
    *   *Conclusion-oriented vs. Decision-oriented*: Free to conceptualize/redesign as one proceeds vs. geared to the needs of a decision-maker [27].

### Research Approaches
*   **Two Basic Approaches**:
    1.  *Quantitative*: Generation of data in quantitative form which can be subjected to rigorous quantitative analysis [28]. Sub-classified into:
        *   *Inferential*: Survey-based approach where a sample is studied to infer population characteristics [28].
        *   *Experimental*: Greater control over the environment; some variables are manipulated to observe their effect [28].
        *   *Simulation*: Construction of an artificial environment within which relevant information/data can be generated [28].
    2.  *Qualitative*: Subjective assessment of attitudes, opinions, and behavior. Uses techniques like focus group interviews, projective techniques, and depth interviews [26, 28].

### Significance of Research
*   **Progress & Inquiry**: "All progress is born of inquiry. Doubt is often better than overconfidence, for it leads to inquiry, and inquiry leads to invention" (Hudson Maxim) [29].
*   **Inductive Thinking**: Inculcates scientific and inductive thinking and promotes logical habits of thinking [29].
*   **Government Policy**: Serves as a vital tool for economic and social policy. Government research operates in three phases: (i) Investigation of economic structure, (ii) Diagnosis of events taking place, and (iii) Prognosis (prediction of future developments) [30].
*   **Operational Problems**: Provides a quantitative basis for decisions in business and industry [29, 30].
*   **Social Value**: Serves as a career path (degree), source of livelihood (professionals), outlet for new ideas (philosophers), developer of style (literary men), and generalizer of new theories (intellectuals) [31, 32].

| Feature | Research Methods | Research Methodology |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

*   **Research Methods**: The methods/techniques used by the researcher to perform research operations (grouped as: data collection, statistical tools for establishing relationships, and methods to evaluate accuracy) [34, 35].
*   **Research Methodology**: A way to systematically solve the research problem. It is the science of studying how research is done scientifically [36]. It includes not only the methods but also the logic behind them, their assumptions, and the criteria for choosing specific techniques [36].

### Research and Scientific Method
*   **Philosophy**: Scientific method is the pursuit of truth as determined by logical considerations [37].
*   **Unity of Science**: "The unity of all sciences consists alone in its methods, not its material" (Karl Pearson) [37].
*   **Aims**: To achieve a systematic interrelation of facts by experimentation, observation, and logical arguments from accepted postulates [37].

### Importance of Knowing How Research is Done
*   **Training**: Provides training in gathering material, card-indexing, fieldwork, statistics, questionnaires, and interpretation [40].
*   **Objectivity**: Helps develop disciplined thinking or a 'bent of mind' to observe fields objectively [41].
*   **Intelligent Consumption**: Enables the user to intelligently evaluate and judge the adequacy of research results and make rational decisions [42].

### Criteria of Good Research
*   **Scientific Standards**:
    1.  Purpose should be clearly defined and common concepts used [63].
    2.  Research procedure should be described in sufficient detail to permit replication [63].
    3.  Procedural design should be carefully planned to yield objective results [63].
    4.  Flaws in design should be reported frankly along with estimates of their effects [63].
    5.  Analysis of data should be adequate and methods of analysis appropriate; validity/reliability checked [64].
    6.  Conclusions should be confined to those justified by data [64].
    7.  Integrity and experience of the researcher warrant greater confidence [64].
*   **Qualities**:
    *   *Systematic*: Structured with specified steps [65].
    *   *Logical*: Guided by logical reasoning (induction/deduction) [65].
    *   *Empirical*: Related to concrete data from real situations [66].
    *   *Replicable*: Allows results to be verified by repeating the study [66].

    9.  Ambiguities in conceptualization and definitions [71].

## Chapter 2: Defining the Research Problem
### What is a Research Problem?
*   **Definition**: A difficulty experienced by a researcher (individual/group) in a practical or theoretical situation, seeking a solution [74].
*   **Components of a Problem**:
    1.  An individual or a group experiencing some difficulty [77].
    2.  Objective(s) to be attained [77].
    3.  Alternative means (courses of action) for obtaining the objective(s) [77].
    4.  Doubt in the mind of the researcher regarding the relative efficiency of alternatives [78].
    5.  An environment to which the difficulty pertains [78].

### Selecting the Problem
*   **Guiding Principles**:
    *   Avoid overdone subjects where throwing new light is difficult [80].
    *   Controversial subjects should not be chosen by an average researcher [80].
    *   Avoid too narrow or too vague problems [80].
    *   The subject must be familiar, feasible, and within reach of resources [80].
    *   Consider researcher's background, training, budget, time, and participant cooperation [81].
    *   Precede selection with a preliminary feasibility study [82].

### Necessity of Defining the Problem
*   **Value**: "A problem clearly stated is a problem half solved" [83].
*   **Function**: Limits scope, separates relevant from irrelevant data, and dictates the research design and analytical techniques to be used [83, 84].

### Technique Involved in Defining a Problem
```text
                  THE 5-STEP PROBLEM DEFINING TECHNIQUE
                                    │
 1. Statement of Problem in a General Way (Broad Query)
                                    │
                                    ▼
 2. Understanding Nature & Origin of Problem (Discussion & Context)
                                    │
                                    ▼
 3. Surveying Available Literature (Reviewing Existing Studies)
                                    │
                                    ▼
 4. Developing Ideas through Discussion (Experience Survey)
                                    │
                                    ▼
 5. Rephrasing into Working Proposition (Operational Definition)
```

*   **Sequential Steps**:
    1.  *Statement of the problem in a general way*: Expressing the practical/intellectual interest with pilot survey if needed [86].
    2.  *Understanding the nature of the problem*: Discussing its origin and objectives with those who raised it [87].
    3.  *Surveying the available literature*: Reviewing conceptual and empirical studies to find theoretical gaps and narrow the focus [88].
    4.  *Developing the ideas through discussions (experience survey)*: Seeking advice from experienced colleagues to sharpen focus [89].
    5.  *Rephrasing the research problem*: Defining technical terms, making basic assumptions explicit, and putting the problem in highly specific operational terms [90, 92].

### An Illustration
*   *General*: "Why is productivity in Japan so much higher than in India?" [93]
*   *Narrowed*: "What factors were responsible for the higher labour productivity of Japan's manufacturing industries during the decade 1971 to 1980 relative to India's manufacturing industries?" [94]
*   *Operational*: "To what extent did labour productivity in 1971 to 1980 in Japan exceed that of India in respect of 15 selected manufacturing industries? What factors were responsible for the productivity differentials between the two countries by industries?" [94]

### Conclusion
*   Defining a problem follows a sequential pattern of clarifying ambiguities, resulting in a realistic, analytically meaningful, and operationally viable proposition [96].

## Chapter 3: Research Design
### Meaning of Research Design
*   **Definition**: The blueprint for the collection, measurement, and analysis of data; arrangement of conditions to combine relevance to research purpose with economy in procedure [99].
*   **Parts of Research Design**:
    *   *Sampling design*: Selection of items to be observed [101].
    *   *Observational design*: Conditions under which observations are made [102].
    *   *Statistical design*: Quantitative choices regarding sample size and analysis [102].
    *   *Operational design*: Techniques to carry out specified procedures [102].

### Need for Research Design
*   **Function**: Facilitates smooth sailing of research operations, maximizing information while minimizing expenditures of time, effort, and money [104].

### Features of a Good Design
*   **Attributes**: Flexible, appropriate, efficient, and economical [105].
*   **Standard**: Minimizes bias, maximizes reliability, and yields the smallest experimental error [105].

### Important Concepts Relating to Research Design
*   **Dependent & Independent Variables**:
    *   *Variable*: A concept which can take on different quantitative values [107].
    *   *Continuous Variable*: Can assume any numerical value within a range (e.g., age) [108].
    *   *Discrete Variable*: Expressed only in integers with distinct gaps (e.g., number of children) [108].
    *   *Dependent vs. Independent*: If variable $Y$ is a consequence of variable $X$, $Y$ is dependent and $X$ is independent [108].
*   **Extraneous Variable**: Independent variables not related to the study's purpose but affecting the dependent variable (causing experimental error) [108].
*   **Control**: Minimizing the influence of extraneous variables using design restraints [108].
*   **Confounded Relationship**: When the dependent variable's change is not free from the influence of extraneous variables [108].
*   **Research Hypothesis**: A predictive statement that relates an independent variable to a dependent variable to be tested [109].
*   **Experimental and Non-experimental Hypothesis-Testing Research**:
    *   *Experimental*: The independent variable is manipulated [109].
    *   *Non-experimental*: The independent variable is NOT manipulated (e.g., correlation of IQ and reading ability) [109].
*   **Experimental & Control Groups**: Exposed to special/novel conditions (experimental) vs. usual/baseline conditions (control) [110].
*   **Treatments**: The different conditions under which groups are placed [110].
*   **Experiment**: Process of examining the truth of a statistical hypothesis (absolute or comparative) [110].
*   **Experimental Unit(s)**: Pre-determined plots or blocks where treatments are applied [111].

### Different Research Designs
| Feature | Exploratory / Formulative | Descriptive / Diagnostic | Hypothesis-Testing (Experimental) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Overall Aim** | Discovering ideas & insights [89]. | Describing characteristics accurately [96]. | Testing causal relationships [119]. |
| **Design Flexibility** | Flexible & non-structured [89]. | Rigid & highly structured [96]. | Rigid with controlled trials [119]. |
| **Sampling Design** | Non-probability (Purposive/Convenience) [89]. | Probability sampling (Random/Stratified) [96]. | Probability sampling & Randomization [119]. |
| **Primary Tools** | Literature survey, Experience survey, Case analysis [89]. | Structured Questionnaires, Observations, Audits [96]. | Controlled Experiments (CRD, RBD, LSD) [119]. |

*   **Exploratory (Formulative) Studies**: Focuses on discovering ideas and insights. Design must be flexible [112]. Three main methods:
    1.  *Survey of concerning literature*: Reviewing existing theoretical/empirical works [113].
    2.  *Experience survey*: Interviewing competent people with practical experience [114].
    3.  *Analysis of 'insight-stimulating' examples*: Intensive study of selected striking instances [115].
*   **Descriptive and Diagnostic Studies**:
    *   *Descriptive*: Portrays characteristics of individuals, groups, or situations [117].
    *   *Diagnostic*: Determines the frequency of something or its association with something else [117].
    *   *Design*: Must be rigid (not flexible) to protect against bias and maximize reliability. Relies on structured instruments and pre-planned statistical computations [117, 119, 120].
*   **Hypothesis-Testing Studies**: Experimental designs permitting causal inferences while minimizing bias [106].

### Basic Principles of Experimental Designs
```text
                 THREE BASIC PRINCIPLES OF EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
                                       │
    ┌──────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────┐
    ▼                          ▼                          ▼
1. REPLICATION             2. RANDOMIZATION          3. LOCAL CONTROL
(Repeating treatments      (Assigning treatments     (Grouping into homogeneous
 to increase precision)     randomly to eliminate    blocks to isolate & reduce
                            extraneous bias)          experimental error)
```

*   **Fisher's Three Principles**:
    1.  *Principle of Replication*: Repeating the experiment more than once to apply treatments to multiple units, thereby increasing statistical accuracy and precision [123].
    2.  *Principle of Randomization*: Randomly assigning treatments to experimental units to safeguard against extraneous variables and assure objective probability measurements [124].
    3.  *Principle of Local Control*: Dividing units into homogeneous blocks and varying extraneous factors deliberately to measure and eliminate soil/block variability from experimental error [124].

### Conclusion
*   The selection of design must be decided in advance based on the universe type, study objective, sampling frame, and desired accuracy [137].

### Appendix: Developing a Research Plan
*   **Research Plan Items**: Clear statement of objectives, explicit description of the problem, operational definitions of concepts, method/approach to be used, detailed technical procedures (quantification details), population to be studied (sampling frame), methods of data processing/analysis, results of pilot tests, and time/cost budgets [141, 142, 143, 144].

## Chapter 4: Sampling Design
### Census and Sample Survey
*   **Census**: Complete enumeration of all items in the population. It is expensive, time-consuming, prone to non-sampling bias, and often physically impossible [145].
*   **Sample Survey**: Examining a representative subset (sample) of the population. Yields sufficiently accurate results with less cost, time, and energy [145].

### Implications of a Sample Design
*   **Sampling Design**: A definite plan determined before data collection to obtain a sample from a given population [127].

### Steps in Sampling Design
1.  *Type of Universe*: Define the set of objects to be studied (Finite vs. Infinite) [146].
2.  *Sampling Unit*: Choose geographic (district), social (family), construction (house), or individual units [147].
3.  *Source List (Sampling Frame)*: Prepare/acquire a reliable, comprehensive, and representative list of items of the universe [147].
4.  *Size of Sample*: Choose an optimum sample size ensuring efficiency, representativeness, reliability, and cost-effectiveness [148].
5.  *Parameters of Interest*: Determine whether estimating population mean, variance, or proportion [148].
6.  *Budgetary Constraint*: Account for funds available for sampling [148].
7.  *Sampling Procedure*: Decide the actual type of sample selection [127].

### Criteria of Selecting a Sampling Procedure
*   **Errors in Sampling**: Minimize systematic bias (caused by inappropriate frame, non-response, measurement errors) and sampling errors (random fluctuations) [128, 141].

### Characteristics of a Good Sample Design
*   Must result in a truly representative sample, have small sampling error, be economically viable, control systematic bias, and allow generalization [128].

### Different Types of Sample Designs
*   **Probability vs. Non-probability Sampling**:
    *   *Non-probability*: Deliberate selection based on convenience or judgement. Probaiblity of selection is unknown (Haphazard, convenience, purposive, quota, judgement sampling) [59].
    *   *Probability (Random/Chance)*: Every item has an equal and known chance of selection (Simple random sampling, complex random sampling) [149].

### How to Select a Random Sample?
*   **Lottery Method**: Draw folded slips from a container [149].
*   **Random Number Tables**: Use Tippett's or other standardized tables to read numbers sequentially [150].

### Random Sample from an Infinite Universe
*   Drawn by maintaining control over selection processes where each drawn item does not affect the infinite pool's properties [11].

```text
                             SAMPLING TECHNIQUES
                                      │
              ┌───────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
              ▼                                               ▼
    NON-PROBABILITY SAMPLING                         PROBABILITY SAMPLING
 (Purposive, Convenience, Quota)                 (Simple Random, Systematic,
                                                 Stratified, Cluster, Multi-stage)
```

### Complex Random Sampling Designs
*   **Systematic Sampling**: Selecting every $k^{th}$ item after a random start [130].
*   **Stratified Sampling**: Dividing a heterogeneous population into homogeneous, non-overlapping subpopulations (strata) and drawing a random sample from each stratum [155].
    *   *Proportional*: Strata sample size proportional to strata population size [132].
    *   *Optimum (Disproportionate)*: Strata sample size based on standard deviation and cost ($n_i \propto N_i \sigma_i$) [152].
*   **Cluster & Area Sampling**: Dividing population into clusters, randomly selecting clusters, and completely enumerating all units within chosen clusters [137].
*   **Multi-stage Sampling**: Sampling in successive stages (e.g., district $\rightarrow$ town $\rightarrow$ household) [138].
*   **Sequential Sampling**: The sample size is not fixed in advance; sampling continues based on cumulative intermediate results [140].

## Chapter 5: Measurement and Scaling Techniques
### Measurement in Research
*   **Definition**: The process of assigning numbers to physical objects or abstract concepts according to rules of correspondence mapping a domain onto a range [155, 156].

### Measurement Scales
*   **Four Types**:
    1.  *Nominal*: Categorical classification with no quantitative value or order (e.g., Gender: 0 for male, 1 for female) [156].
    2.  *Ordinal*: Ranks items in order of magnitude (relative positions) but distances between positions are unequal or unknown (e.g., military ranks) [152].
    3.  *Interval*: Ranks with equal, standardized intervals between positions but has an arbitrary zero point (e.g., Fahrenheit temperature) [152].
    4.  *Ratio*: Ranks with equal intervals and an absolute, true zero point representing absence of the trait (e.g., weight, height) [152].

### Sources of Error in Measurement
*   **Four Factors**:
    1.  *Respondent*: Fatigue, boredom, anxiety, or ignorance [161].
    2.  *Situation*: Presence of others, noise, or lack of privacy [161].
    3.  *Measurer*: Interviewer bias, rewording, or improper coding [120, 161].
    4.  *Instrument*: Defective layout, ambiguous wording, or formatting issues [162].

### Tests of Sound Measurement
*   **Three Key Criteria**:
    1.  *Validity*: Extent to which an instrument measures what it claims to measure [157].
        *   *Content*: Adequate coverage of the topic (judgmental/intuitive) [158].
        *   *Criterion-Related (Predictive/Concurrent)*: Ability to predict or relate to an external criterion [158, 159].
        *   *Construct*: Extent to which it measures a theoretical trait [158].
    2.  *Reliability*: Stability (consistent over time) and Equivalence (consistent across items/investigators) [160].
    3.  *Practicality*: Judged by economy (budget trade-offs), convenience (easy layout), and interpretability (supplemented by manual and scoring keys) [162].

### Technique of Developing Measurement Tools
*   **Four Stages**: (a) Concept development, (b) Specification of concept dimensions, (c) Selection of indicators, and (d) Formation of an index [163].

### Scaling
*   **Definition**: Procedures of assigning numbers to degrees of opinions, attitudes, or institutional concepts along a continuum [167].

### Scale Classification Bases
*   Classified by: (a) Subject orientation, (b) Response form, (c) Degree of subjectivity, (d) Scale properties, (e) Number of dimensions, and (f) Scale construction techniques [168].

### Important Scaling Techniques
*   **Rating Scales**: Absolute evaluation against categories without reference to other objects (e.g., graphic scale or itemized scale) [171].
*   **Ranking Scales**: Relative evaluation comparing objects directly (e.g., Paired Comparison, Method of Equal-Appearing Intervals, or Rank Order) [171, 172].

### Scale Construction Techniques
*   **Five Major Approaches**:
    1.  *Arbitrary*: Ad-hoc, subjective item selection [169].
    2.  *Consensus (Thurstone)*: Submitted to judges who sort statements into 11 piles. Statements with high agreement (low variance) and evenly spread median values constitute the final scale [177, 178].
    3.  *Item Analysis (Likert Summated)*: 5-point agreements scored. Extreme top and bottom 25% groups act as criteria; items with high discriminatory power are retained [179, 180, 181, 182].
    4.  *Cumulative (Guttman Scalogram)*: Unidimensional, structured series where agreement with an extreme item implies agreement with all less extreme items. Evaluated by Coefficient of Reproducibility ($C_R = 1 - e/(nN)$; minimum standard 0.90) [185, 186, 191].
    5.  *Factor Scales*: Based on multidimensional intercorrelations [192].
        *   *Semantic Differential*: Charles Osgood's 7-point bipolar ratings evaluating three dominant dimensions: Evaluation, Potency, and Activity [193, 194].
        *   *Multidimensional Scaling (MDS)*: Plots judged similarities as physical distances in a multidimensional geometric space [198].

## Chapter 6: Methods of Data Collection
### Collection of Primary Data
*   **Primary Data**: Collected afresh and for the first time; original in character [203].

### Observation Method
*   **Definition**: Systematic viewing of raw behavior [96].
*   **Types**: Structured vs. Unstructured [96], Participant vs. Non-participant [96], and Controlled vs. Uncontrolled [97].
*   **Evaluation**: Eliminates subjective bias and record independent of respondent willingness, but is expensive and limited to current behavior [96, 97].

### Interview Method
*   **Personal Interview**: Structured (standardized set of questions) or Unstructured (flexible sequence/wording) [204].
*   **Other Types**: Focussed (focuses on concrete experiences), Clinical (broad motivations), Non-directive (interviewer acts as a passive catalyst), and Telephone [204, 205].
*   **Evaluation**: High response rate, deeper probing, but expensive and prone to interviewer bias [203].

### Collection of Data through Questionnaires
*   **Definition**: Mailed or sent series of typed questions to be completed independently by respondents [208].
*   **Main Aspects**: General form (structured vs. unstructured) [209], Question sequence (general-to-specific; vital questions in middle) [210, 211], and Question formulation (simple, concrete, unambiguous, avoiding "danger/catch" words) [211, 213].
*   **Wording Forms**: Multiple choice (closed) vs. Open-ended (latitude in phrasing) [212].
*   **Essentials**: Short, attractive physical appearance, clear instructions, and includes control questions for verification [214, 215].

### Collection of Data through Schedules
*   **Definition**: Questionnaires filled out in person by trained enumerators [104]. Enumerators must be honest, patient, and trained to cross-examine [216].

### Difference between Questionnaires and Schedules
*   *Questionnaires*: Cheaper, sent by mail, relies on respondent literacy, suffers from high non-response, and has no direct contact [214].
*   *Schedules*: Costly, filled out by enumerators, can use illiterate respondents, low non-response, and permits identity verification [214, 216].

### Some Other Methods of Data Collection
*   **Pantry & Store Audits**: Physical count of stocks [106].
*   **Consumer Panels**: Transcribe continuous diaries of purchases [106].
*   **Projective Techniques**: Indirect assessment of unconscious motivations [108].
    *   *Word Association & Sentence/Story Completion* [217, 218].
    *   *TAT & HIT (Holtzman Inkblot Test)*: HIT uses 45 cards with single responses to assess barrier scores [219, 221].
    *   *Play & Sociometry (sociological patterns)* [222].
*   **Depth Interviews & Content Analysis**: Motivational probing vs. systematic qualitative evaluation of documents [223, 224].

### Collection of Secondary Data
*   **Secondary Data**: Already collected and analyzed by someone else [226].
*   **Criteria for Use**:
    1.  *Reliability*: Who collected it? What methods? Was there bias? [227]
    2.  *Suitability*: Do definitions and scope match the present study? [227]
    3.  *Adequacy*: Accuracy levels and physical coverage boundaries [228].

### Selection of Appropriate Method for Data Collection
*   Depends on the: (i) Nature, scope, and object of enquiry, (ii) Availability of funds, (iii) Time factor, and (iv) Precision required [229].

### Case Study Method
*   **Definition**: Intensive qualitative observation and in-depth analysis of a single social unit (person, family, institution, community) as an integrated totality [230, 231].
*   **Phases**: Locating the status, collecting history, diagnosing causal factors, applying therapy, and follow-up [234, 235].
*   **Assumptions**: Uniformity in basic human nature; completeness of historical sequence [234].

*   **Descriptive**: Largely the study of distributions of one variable (unidimensional), two variables (bivariate), or more (multivariate) [277].

*   **Mean Deviation**: Average of absolute deviations from mean/median/mode [284].
*   **Standard Deviation ($\sigma$)**: Root-mean-square deviation. Gives the measure of dispersion and basis for standard error [135, 303].

### Measures of Asymmetry (Skewness)

## Chapter 8: Sampling Fundamentals
### Need for Sampling & Basic Definitions
*   Sampling is essential when a census is impossible, prohibitively expensive, or destructive.
*   **Population Parameter**: Numerical characteristic of the population (e.g., population mean $\mu$, population standard deviation $\sigma_p$).
*   **Sample Statistic**: Corresponding numerical value calculated from sample observations (e.g., sample mean $\bar{X}$, sample standard deviation $s$).

### Sampling Distributions & Central Limit Theorem
*   **Sampling Distribution**: The probability distribution of a given sample statistic (e.g., $\bar{X}$) obtained from all possible random samples of size $n$ drawn from a population.
*   **Central Limit Theorem**: Regardless of the functional shape of the population distribution, the sampling distribution of the sample mean $\bar{X}$ approaches a normal distribution as the sample size $n$ increases ($n > 30$).

### Concept of Standard Error ($SE$)
*   **Definition**: Standard error is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of a statistic. It serves as a measure of precision for sampling estimates.
*   **Standard Error Formulas**:
    *   *Standard Error of Mean ($\sigma_{\bar{X}}$)*: $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma_p}{\sqrt{n}}$ (when population $\sigma_p$ is known).
    *   *When $\sigma_p$ is unknown*: Use sample standard deviation $s$ as best estimate: $SE_{\bar{X}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$.
    *   *Standard Error of Proportion ($\sigma_p$)*: $\sigma_p = \sqrt{\frac{p \cdot q}{n}}$ where $q = 1 - p$.
    *   *Finite Population Multiplier (FPM)*: When sampling without replacement from a finite population $N$ and $n/N > 0.05$:
        $$\text{FPM} = \sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}} \implies \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma_p}{\sqrt{n}} \times \sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}}$$

### Estimation & Confidence Intervals
*   **Point Estimation**: Single numerical value used to estimate a population parameter.
*   **Interval Estimation**: A range of values within which the population parameter is expected to lie with a specified confidence level ($1 - \alpha$).
*   **Confidence Limits**:
    *   *95% Confidence Interval*: $\bar{X} \pm 1.96 \cdot \sigma_{\bar{X}}$
    *   *99% Confidence Interval*: $\bar{X} \pm 2.58 \cdot \sigma_{\bar{X}}$

### Sample Size Determination Formulas
1.  **Estimating Mean (Infinite Population)**:
    $$n = \frac{z^2 \cdot \sigma_p^2}{e^2} \quad \text{where } e = \text{acceptable margin of error}$$
2.  **Estimating Mean (Finite Population $N$)**:
    $$n = \frac{z^2 \cdot N \cdot \sigma_p^2}{(N-1)e^2 + z^2 \cdot \sigma_p^2}$$
3.  **Estimating Proportion (Infinite Population)**:
    $$n = \frac{z^2 \cdot p \cdot q}{e^2}$$
4.  **Estimating Proportion (Finite Population $N$)**:
    $$n = \frac{z^2 \cdot p \cdot q \cdot N}{(N-1)e^2 + z^2 \cdot p \cdot q}$$

## Chapter 9: Testing of Hypotheses-I (Parametric Tests)
### What is a Hypothesis?
*   A predictive statement or tentative proposition subject to empirical verification.
*   **Characteristics**: Specific, testable, limited in scope, stated in simple terms, and consistent with known facts.

### Basic Concepts
*   **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$)**: The hypothesis of "no difference" or no effect, tested for possible rejection.
*   **Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$)**: The operational statement accepted when $H_0$ is rejected.
*   **Level of Significance ($\alpha$)**: The probability of committing a Type I error (commonly set at 5% or 1%).
*   **Two-Tailed vs. One-Tailed Tests**:
    *   *Two-Tailed*: Rejection region lies on both tails ($H_a: \mu \neq \mu_0$).
    *   *One-Tailed*: Rejection region lies entirely on one tail ($H_a: \mu > \mu_0$ or $H_a: \mu < \mu_0$).

### Decision Errors
```text
                         DECISION ERROR MATRIX
                                    │
                        ACTUAL TRUTH OF NULL HYPOTHESIS (H₀)
                        H₀ is TRUE              H₀ is FALSE
                 ┌───────────────────────┬───────────────────────┐
  ACCEPT H₀      │  CORRECT DECISION     │     TYPE II ERROR     │
                 │  Confidence (1 - α)   │     Beta Error (β)    │
  DECISION       ├───────────────────────┼───────────────────────┤
  REJECT H₀      │     TYPE I ERROR      │  CORRECT DECISION     │
                 │  Alpha Error (α)      │   Power Test (1 - β)  │
                 └───────────────────────┴───────────────────────┘
```

### Flow Diagram for Hypothesis Testing
```text
               ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐
               │   1. State Null (H₀) & Alternative (H␁)  │
               └────────────────────┬─────────────────────┘
                                    │
                                    ▼
               ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐
               │ 2. Specify Level of Significance (α)     │
               └────────────────────┬─────────────────────┘
                                    │

               ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐
               │ 3. Decide Correct Sampling Distribution │
               │          (z, t, F, or χ²)                │
               └────────────────────┬─────────────────────┘
                                    │
                                    ▼
               ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐
               │ 4. Sample Random Data & Compute Statistic│
               └────────────────────┬─────────────────────┘
                                    │
                                    ▼

               └────────────────────┬─────────────────────┘
                                    │
                                    ▼

               │ 6. Is Probability ≤ α (or α/2)?          │

               ┌────────────────────┐┌────────────────────┐

               │ (Risk: Type I Error)││ (Risk: Type II Error)
               └────────────────────┘└────────────────────┘
```

### Important Parametric Tests
1.  **$z$-Test (Normal Distribution)**:
    *   *Single Mean*: $z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\sigma_p / \sqrt{n}}$
    *   *Difference of Means*: $z = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}}$
    *   *Single Proportion*: $z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{\frac{p_0 q_0}{n}}}$
    *   *Difference of Proportions*: $z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{p q \left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}
\right)}}$
2.  **$t$-Test (Student's $t$-Distribution, $n \le 30$)**:
    *   *Single Mean*: $t = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}$ with $df = n - 1$.
    *   *Difference of Means*: $t = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{\sqrt{s_p^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}
\right)}}$ where $s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}$ with $df = n_1 + n_2 - 2$.
    *   *Paired $t$-Test*: $t = \frac{\bar{D}}{\sigma_{\text{diff}} / \sqrt{n}}$ with $df = n - 1$.
3.  **Sandler's $A$-Test**:
    *   A simplified, algebraically equivalent alternative to the paired $t$-test using difference scores:
        $$A = \frac{\sum D_i^2}{\left(\sum D_i\right)^2}$$
4.  **$F$-Test (Variance Ratio Test)**:
    *   Compares the equality of two population variances:
        $$F = \frac{S_1^2}{S_2^2} \quad (S_1^2 > S_2^2) \quad df_1 = n_1 - 1, df_2 = n_2 - 1$$

## Chapter 10: Chi-Square Test
### Meaning & Nature of $\chi^2$ Test
*   A non-parametric statistical test used to evaluate the agreement between observed frequencies ($O_{ij}$) and expected frequencies ($E_{ij}$) based on a theoretical distribution or hypothesis.

### Key Applications
1.  **Test of Goodness of Fit**: Tests whether observed sample frequencies conform to a specific theoretical distribution (e.g., Normal, Binomial, Poisson).
2.  **Test of Independence of Attributes**: Evaluates whether two categorical variables in a contingency table are independent or associated.

### Essential Conditions for Applying $\chi^2$ Test
1. Observations must be collected on a random sampling basis.
2. All items in the sample must be independent.
3. No cell group should have an expected frequency less than 10 (or 5; adjacent cells must be combined if smaller).
4. Overall sample size $N$ must be reasonably large ($N \ge 50$).
5. Linear constraints on cell frequencies.

### Formula & Procedure
$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r \sum_{j=1}^c \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}$$
*   *Expected Frequency Formula*: $E_{ij} = \frac{\text{Row } i \text{ Total} \times \text{Column } j \text{ Total}}{N}$
*   *Degrees of Freedom*: $df = (r - 1)(c - 1)$

### $2 \times 2$ Contingency Grid & Yates' Correction
```text
                         2x2 CONTINGENCY GRID
                               Column 1      Column 2        Total
                Row 1             a             b            a + b
                Row 2             c             d            c + d
                Total           a + c         b + d            N
```

*   **Alternative Cross-Product Formula**: $\chi^2 = \frac{N (ad - bc)^2}{(a + b)(c + d)(a + c)(b + d)}$
*   **Yates' Continuity Correction**: Adjusts for continuous $\chi^2$ approximation of discrete frequencies by subtracting 0.5 from deviations:
    $$\chi^2_{\text{corrected}} = \frac{N \left(|ad - bc| - 0.5 N\right)^2}{(a + b)(c + d)(a + c)(b + d)}$$

## Chapter 11: Analysis of Variance and Covariance
### What is ANOVA?
*   A powerful statistical technique developed by R.A. Fisher to test whether the means of three or more populations are equal simultaneously.

### Basic Principle
*   ANOVA partitions the total sum of squares ($SS_{\text{total}}$) into two independent components:
    1.  *Sum of Squares Between Samples ($SS_{\text{between}}$)*: Measures variation due to treatment effects.
    2.  *Sum of Squares Within Samples ($SS_{\text{within}}$)*: Measures random experimental error.

### Standard One-Way ANOVA Table
| Source of Variation | Sum of Squares ($SS$) | Degrees of Freedom ($df$) | Mean Square ($MS$) | $F$-Ratio |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Between Samples (Treatments)** | $SS_{\text{between}}$ | $k - 1$ | $MS_{\text{between}} = \frac{SS_{\text{between}}}{k - 1}$ | $F = \frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{within}}}$ |
| **Within Samples (Error)** | $SS_{\text{within}}$ | $n - k$ | $MS_{\text{within}} = \frac{SS_{\text{within}}}{n - k}$ | *(Compare with Critical $F$)* |
| **Total** | $SS_{\text{total}}$ | $n - 1$ | | |

### Two-Way ANOVA Table
| Source of Variation | Sum of Squares ($SS$) | Degrees of Freedom ($df$) | Mean Square ($MS$) | $F$-Ratio |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Between Columns** | $SS_c$ | $c - 1$ | $MS_c = \frac{SS_c}{c - 1}$ | $F_c = \frac{MS_c}{MS_e}$ |
| **Between Rows** | $SS_r$ | $r - 1$ | $MS_r = \frac{SS_r}{r - 1}$ | $F_r = \frac{MS_r}{MS_e}$ |
| **Residual (Error)** | $SS_e$ | $(c - 1)(r - 1)$ | $MS_e = \frac{SS_e}{(c - 1)(r - 1)}$ | |
| **Total** | $SS_{\text{total}}$ | $n - 1$ | | |

### Latin Square Design (LSD)
*   An experimental design used to control two extraneous sources of variation simultaneously by arranging treatments such that each treatment occurs exactly once in each row and column ($v \times v$ square).

### Analysis of Covariance (ANOCOVA)
*   Combines linear regression with ANOVA to remove the effect of an uncontrolled continuous variable (covariate $Z$) that is correlated with the dependent variable $Y$.
*   Adjusted degrees of freedom for error become $N - k - 1$.

## Chapter 12: Testing of Hypotheses-II (Nonparametric Tests)
### Parametric vs. Nonparametric Comparison
| Feature | Parametric Tests | Nonparametric Tests |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Distribution Assumptions** | Assumes underlying normal population distribution [15]. | Distribution-free; no normality assumption required [15]. |
| **Measurement Scale** | Interval or Ratio scale data [15]. | Nominal or Ordinal scale data [15]. |
| **Statistical Power** | Higher statistical efficiency/power when assumptions hold [313]. | Slightly lower power; requires larger sample for same power [313]. |
| **Sample Size** | Preferred for large samples [15]. | Highly effective for small sample sizes [15]. |

### Important Nonparametric Tests
1.  **One-Sample & Two-Sample Sign Test**: Based on the direction of plus ($+$) or minus ($-$) signs rather than numerical magnitudes.
2.  **McNemar Test**: Used for paired dichotomous data in nominal before-after designs.
3.  **Wilcoxon Matched-Pairs Signed-Rank Test**: Evaluates both direction and magnitude of differences in paired ordinal data.
4.  **Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney $U$-Test**: Evaluates whether two independent samples come from the same continuous population:
    $$U_1 = n_1 n_2 + \frac{n_1(n_1 + 1)}{2} - R_1, \quad U_2 = n_1 n_2 + \frac{n_2(n_2 + 1)}{2} - R_2, \quad U = \min(U_1, U_2)$$
5.  **Kruskal-Wallis $H$-Test**: Nonparametric alternative to One-Way ANOVA on ranked data:
    $$H = \frac{12}{n(n + 1)} \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{R_i^2}{n_i} - 3(n + 1) \quad df = k - 1$$
6.  **One-Sample Runs Test**: Tests randomness of a sequence of dichotomous observations.
7.  **Kendall's Coefficient of Concordance ($W$)**: Measures degree of agreement among $k$ sets of rankings across $N$ objects:
    $$W = \frac{12 s}{k^2(N^3 - N)} \quad \text{where } s = \sum (R_j - \bar{R})^2$$

### Classification of Multivariate Techniques

### Dependence Methods
1.  **Multiple Regression Analysis**: Predicts a single continuous metric dependent variable $Y$ from multiple independent variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k$.
2.  **Multiple Discriminant Analysis**: Predicts a categorical non-metric dependent variable from several metric predictor variables ($D^2$ statistic).
3.  **Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA)**: Extension of ANOVA evaluating group differences across several metric dependent variables simultaneously.
4.  **Canonical Correlation Analysis**: Simultaneously correlates a set of continuous criterion variables with a set of explanatory variables.

### Interdependence Methods & Factor Analysis
*   **Factor Analysis**: Condenses a large set of correlated variables into a smaller set of latent factors.
    *   *Centroid Method (Thurstone)*: Extracts factors based on average column sums of correlation matrix $R$.
    *   *Principal Components Method (Hotelling)*: Maximizes sum of squared factor loadings to account for maximum variance.
*   **Communality ($h^2$)**: Total proportion of variance in variable $j$ explained by $m$ common factors:
    $$h_j^2 = \sum_{i=1}^m a_{ji}^2$$
*   **Eigenvalue**: Sum of squared factor loadings across all variables for a given factor.
*   **Rotation**: Rotating factor axes (Varimax orthogonal, Promax oblique) to achieve Thurstone's simple structure for unambiguous interpretation.
*   **Path Analysis**: Visual path diagrams utilizing standardized beta weights to decompose total correlations into direct and indirect causal effects.
